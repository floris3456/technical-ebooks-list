# Introduction to Algorithms — Fourth edition

## Recommendation and audience

**Recommend as a broad algorithms reference and structured foundation in algorithm design and analysis.** MIT Press identifies the fourth edition, all four authors, and publication on April 5, 2022. The electronic ISBN is distinct from the hardcover identifier. The publisher does not establish a PDF/EPUB subtype in the inspected listing; that metadata is left unknown without affecting selection. [S1]

The intermediate classification is our judgment for readers who have programmed and are prepared to work with mathematical arguments. JHU's course resources explicitly direct readers needing preparation to discrete mathematics and proof material. It is not positioned here as a first coding tutorial. [S1], [S3]

## Contents and subject fit

The official contents establish substantial coverage of analysis, divide-and-conquer, data structures, dynamic programming, greedy methods, graph algorithms, and complexity. For orientation, fourth-edition chapter 14 covers dynamic programming, chapter 16 amortized analysis, and chapter 20 elementary graph algorithms. [S2]

Keep the primary subject **Algorithms & Data Structures**. A mathematics appendix and a machine-learning chapter do not, by themselves, make this a general mathematics or machine-learning recommendation. This is a deliberate classification judgment from the scope, not a denial that those connections exist. [S2]

## Why it remains relevant today

Johns Hopkins' Fall 2026 Introduction to Algorithms resources explicitly name the fourth edition as the basis for most lectures. The schedule assigns asymptotic analysis, balanced trees, amortized analysis, hashing, and dynamic programming during September–October. This ties the recommendation to identifiable current teaching tasks, not merely a reputation accumulated by earlier editions. [S3], [S4]

The value sought is understanding algorithms and reasoning about their costs, rather than memorizing a particular library API. The publisher's use of pseudocode and the verified contents support that positioning. Course use corroborates relevance but does not establish that this is the easiest or best book for every reader. [S1], [S2], [S3]

## Limitations and alternatives

Broad coverage and mathematical analysis are strengths for reference use but can make an unstructured cover-to-cover plan inappropriate for a newcomer; that is our audience-fit assessment. No complete reading, exercise completion, or comparative learning trial was performed in this cycle. Actual scope was checked through the official contents, not through every chapter's full text. [S1], [S2], [S3]

JHU also lists Kleinberg and Tardos's *Algorithm Design* and Motwani and Raghavan's *Randomized Algorithms* as further reading. They are useful comparison leads, not automatically approved substitutes or additional recommendations. This record selects CLRS for its verified broad scope; a subsequent cycle should compare more focused alternatives directly. [S3]

## Edition, errata, and evidence caveats

The inspected publisher listing is for the fourth edition. The official errata interface is printing-specific; its text rendering exposed the controls but not a complete selected correction list. Do not interpret that as an error-free book or claim that all corrections were audited. [S1], [S5]

The JHU schedule calls itself preliminary. Some later-semester chapter references do not match the fourth-edition contents: for example, it labels single-source shortest paths chapter 24, whereas the official fourth-edition contents put it in chapter 22. The explicit edition statement in the resources remains useful adoption evidence, but the publisher's contents—not all schedule numbers—are authoritative for edition-specific navigation. [S2], [S3], [S4]
